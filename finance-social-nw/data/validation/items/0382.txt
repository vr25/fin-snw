Deachman: If you're in Canada, you can't see this article on Facebook. You can't share news at all. Even if your town is on fire.
Given Canadians’ reliance on them, do social media platforms have an obligation to maintain that information flow, and should they help pay for it?
As northern wildfires forced the evacuation of Yellowknife, journalists at Cabin Radio, an independent web-based radio station and newsroom in the Northwest Territories capital, have been trying to keep residents informed about the rapidly evolving situation.
Those efforts have been hindered by Facebook, which blocked all news stories from Canadians’ feeds on its platform. So not only was Cabin Radio unable to post its stories there, residents who wanted to share stories from the news organization’s website with their friends and family via Facebook were similarly prevented from doing so.
“I’ve seen a lot of misinformation going out on social media,” Camp Radio reporter and assistant editor Emily Blake told Global News. “The fact that we can’t share news links on Facebook is making that incredibly difficult to address.”
The situation in Yellowknife is indicative of Canadians’ reliance on internet tech behemoths for finding and sharing news, especially in smaller communities. And what happens in Yellowknife won’t necessarily stay in Yellowknife. Imagine the same blackout during a derecho here in Ottawa or a protracted mass shooting like the one that occurred in Nova Scotia in 2020.
Given Canadians’ reliance on them, do social media platforms such as Facebook have an obligation to maintain that information flow, and should they help pay for it?
It seems the government thinks so. Facebook’s decision to block news posts from its platform, and a similar threat from Google, are in response to the federal government’s Online News Act, which received royal assent in June. Many of the act’s regulatory details have yet to be determined, and enforcement won’t begin until December, but Facebook is already flexing its considerable muscle against it.
Directed at “digital news intermediaries” (DNI) Facebook and Google, who are owned by Meta and Alphabet, respectively, the act aims to take some of the money those platforms have made from sharing news stories and put it into the pockets of the organizations that created the content in the first place.
It’s based on The News Media Bargaining Code that Australia enacted in 2021 to coerce Google and Facebook into making deals with news organizations or face binding arbitration. When it came into force there, Facebook similarly blocked news stories from appearing, but its approach was heavy-handed — it included government health sites and, coincidentally, news about wildfires that imperilled citizens Down Under — creating an enormous PR backlash. Facebook reversed course within a week and began to negotiate with news organizations, as did Google.
In Canada, a standoff exists, with neither the government nor big tech appearing to be willing to back down. The social media platforms have framed the legislation as a “link tax,” saying they shouldn’t have to pay for them since, they argue, the links actually are more valuable to the news organization as they redirect readers to their websites.
The wholesale blocking of news by Facebook doesn’t make a distinction between the Globe & Mail, for example, and Nairobi’s Daily Nation. Only by eliminating all news stories from its platform can Meta, and Google if the latter does likewise, avoid qualifying as DNIs subject to the law.
Are Meta and Google bluffing? Maybe not. The Australian experience saw the tech companies sign deals with news organizations worth $200 million (AUD) annually, or about $175 million Cdn, while Canada’s Parliamentary Budget Office estimated the act would bring in more than $300 million annually for news organizations here. These figures may be pittances for such large corporations, but, if the tech companies agree to negotiate in Canada, why wouldn’t they have to follow suit in other, much larger markets, such as the United States and European Union, where similar legislative efforts are already afoot? Canada, then, might be where they draw the line in the sand.
Complicating the issue are concerns such as whether news media should share such close relationships with governments or big tech, which could appear to compromise the independence of the press. Others have asked whether social media platforms that have been successful in capturing advertising revenue should be on the hook for the failures of legacy media. According to Canadian Heritage, overall revenue for Broadcast television, radio, newspapers and magazines fell by almost $6 billion between 2008 and 2020, while more than one-third of journalism jobs disappeared between 2010 and 2016. Since 2008, nearly 500 media outlets have shuttered across Canada.
You could argue that that’s the way the cookie crumbles, at least if companies such as Facebook and Google had taken over the mantle of news reporting.
But they haven’t.
Instead, they are using the information created by journalists to help attract eyes to their platforms, where users are both the product and the market, and help the companies refine algorithms and sell advertising. Google, meanwhile, is hoovering just about everything on the internet, including news stories, as it continues to develop Artificial Intelligence. So it’s disingenuous of them to suggest they’re not financially benefitting from the work of others.
Brent Jolly, president of the Canadian Association of Journalists, sees the legislation as a way to balance the two sides.
“I think what we’ve seen from Australia, what my concern is, are journalists employed?” He told me. “Are they able to do their job effectively and without interference? And is the public’s right to know being upheld? And I think that all of those things are at least alluded to or contained in the legislation.”
Few would argue against a healthy media sector. The passage of Australia’s legislation, according to Rod Sims, who chaired the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) that developed that country’s code, was eased by Australians’ understanding of the importance of a vibrant press.
“We justified what we did at the ACCC on the basis that, look, there are a whole lot of entities that Google and Facebook don’t fund, but the media is really important,” he told me in a phone call from Sydney. “So you’ve really got to sort this out because a well-functioning press is fundamental to democracy. And that argument really caught on here. I think most people accept that we can’t have a functioning democracy without a vibrant media sector.”
I asked Sims if faith in media might be higher in Australia than in North America, where in recent years it’s been under almost constant attack. He couldn’t say, except to point out that Australia and the U.S. are not nearly as similar as they once were.
But he said the experience there, now well into its second year, had been a success. “You had journalists saying there’s never been a better time to be a journalist. You had academics noticing an increased demand for courses. The number of job ads for journalists rose by nearly 50 per cent once the deals had been done.”
Some experts, however, don’t think that Meta and Google will come to the table in Canada.
“I really do believe Meta is walking away here,” University of Ottawa law professor Michael Geist told me. “I see no reason to think it’s a bluff.
“The data speaks for itself, as long as you believe their data: News is about three per cent of users’ feeds, and that’s been declining. They’ve been de-emphasizing it because it’s way more of a headache than it’s worth to them. And they say that it’s highly substitutable; people spend the same amount of time on that platform whether they’re looking at pictures of friends or clicking on news links.”
If that’s the case, Geist says the Act is a lose-lose-lose-lose proposition where the government gets nothing it hoped for by enacting it; news organizations, particularly smaller ones that had found ways to use social media to their benefit, will see no monetary benefit and actually lose traffic to their sites, while media that had previously inked deals with the platforms will have those cancelled; tech platforms will see their products diminished; and users, especially communities that depend on Facebook, will suffer.
People, meanwhile, are already finding workarounds to Facebook’s news embargo, including posting screen grabs from news stories.
It’s difficult to imagine that Google wants to become a search engine without news, but it’s harder to guess what Meta will do. If the void it has created in the dissemination of news continues, media organizations may take their lumps in the short term, but then it’s an opportunity to attract more readers directly. That might require resources they don’t currently possess.
The government could still help without directly tying media groups to social platforms by using some of the money it intends to collect through its proposed digital services tax — a three-per-cent levy on revenues of social media platforms — and, through an arm’s length group, use it to bolster its Canadian Journalism Labour Tax Credit or other programs that could encourage media growth.
If you believe that a healthy vibrant press is a public good and one that benefits democracy, it should be treated like one. If you don’t, well, what happens when the fires are at your door?
