Chinese-owned TikTok will be banned from all government devices under a measure included in the $1.7 trillion omnibus spending bill that lawmakers must pass this week.
The company, which is already not allowed by multiple agencies including the Pentagon, State Department, Transporation Security Administration and Department of Homeland Security, has faced a torrent of criticism on the idea that all the data it collects can be shared with the Chinese government.
For its part, TikTok has always maintained that it protects users' data and would not share it with China's communist government - despite recent revelations that contradict their statements.
The inclusion of the ban comes after the Senate passed the governmentwide ban measure separately last week. In addition, at least 19 states have passed similar bans on TikTok for devices issues by state governments to their employees.

'TikTok will soon be BANNED on all government devices,' Republican Sen. Josh Hawley of Missouri tweeted on Tuesday.
TikTok, which has over a billion monthly active users and is owned by China-based ByteDance, has become wildly popular worldwide and especially among coveted younger users in America.
'People are catching up to the fact that China is a hostile power,' James Lewis, senior vice president and director of the strategic technologies program at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, told The Washington Post.
Lewis noted that China has banned American-developed apps like Facebook and Twitter. 'TikTok is caught up in that — guilty or not,' he added.
Since 2019, the video-sharing app has been negotiating an agreement with the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS), a government group that can cancel business deals if they pose national security concerns.
Four people with knowledge of those discussions told Post on that TikTok has agreed to sever decision-making over its U.S. operations from ByteDance, and to give U.S. authorities veto power over the appointment of the company’s proposed three-member board and its top executives.

The sources also told the Post that U.S. officials would set the standards for the hiring of TikTok’s American staff, which would subject the company to far more aggressive government oversight than any U.S. technology firm faces.
Silicon Valley companies like Google, Meta, Apple and Amazon, and their hired lobbyists, did manage to prevent any major privacy and antitrust measures from being including in the massive year-end spending bill.
The industry has spent more than $100 million since the beginning of 2021 on advertisements opposing such legislation. They also funneled an additional $130 million on advertisements in key swing states.
Two measures that could have been included in the spending bill were more targeted. One would enhance children's online privacy protections and the other would limit the gatekeeper powers of companies like Apple and Google over smartphone apps.
