My ancestors navigated their world by the stars. All our ancestors did, scanning the skies, smelling the wind, remembering a cliff, a river confluence, or a village.
Ancient Hawaiians navigated from the most remote islands in the world to the shore of South America, Rapa Nui, Easter Island where they would leave huge statues remembering their people. Wayfinders would be sent out to set direction for the village, often by prayer, and with the wisdom of observations. We remember less these days.

The swans and herons, however, remember where they are supposed to go, navigating tens of thousands of miles to land back on our lakes. Amazing as it may seem, some of life continues without Google Maps.
Times have changed for some, and Grandfather Google is the answer. Often in our traditional ceremonies, people talk about speaking with the Grandfathers and Grandmothers, but today, we speak to Grandfather Google.
In today’s world many people will not venture far from the security of their home without some form of electronic navigation. We check our phones enroute to whatever destination, as if we need to immediately respond. I am guilty of this as well. People no longer ask me about maple syrup, or maybe my gardens, but instead if I saw their latest post or someone’s clever post on Facebook.
Then there’s the City People. They seem incapable of basic directions, like “take a right after 5 miles at the store, or third house on the left.” Most definitely will not move without the phone and an electronic address.
We’ve come pretty far, and I don’t know that we are much smarter for it.
Then the questions: Grandfather Google is the go-to for so many essential and nonessential questions. What was this called, what’s the recipe for cornbread? And more. Many of us no longer use pen and paper, but instead use the “wtf, lol” way to communicate. The schools, I have heard, are no longer teaching cursive writing – the writing of our elders. Instead, young people are learning much more electronically. Frankly, the idea that we will not be able to read the writing of our grandparents concerns me deeply.
It’s a rather expensive habit and its very invisibility obscures some hard facts. In three scant years, Google’s energy consumption increased from 12.7 terawatt hours in 2019 to 22.3 by 2022, almost a doubling of use. As a comparison, in 2002, total U.S. electrical use was 3.3 terawatts.

And that’s just the beginning. Asking Grandfather Google a question takes energy. And while Google and its competitors are working to be carbon negative, the demand of the internet giants is gobbling up renewable energy production. According to Forbes Magazine, Amazon has said it expects renewable energy to power 100% of its operations by 2025. Google aims to achieve net-zero emissions across all of its operations by 2030, reported Yahoo! Finance. That’s a lot of wind turbines and solar panels.
Then add to that cryptocurrency. That’s one of the most interesting and bizarre evolutions to me, the development of new money systems which are created in cyberspace.
Cryptocurrency “mines” are specialized data centers set up to run complex algorithms that mint new digital tokens. The industry has rapidly grown into one of the most energy-intensive in the world — an estimated 150 terawatt-hours of electricity annually, more than the entire country of Argentina, according to a University of Cambridge study. And now they are coming to North Dakota if all works out.
Supplanting the ecologically crazy idea of fracking with cryptocurrency facilities in the state doesn’t make better neighbors. Calling it “cleanest crypto on the planet.” North Dakota officials are marketing hard, when in fact North Dakota has one of the dirtiest power mixes, largely a result of coal generation and the fracking economy.
Maybe we want to ask what and why we power? Is streaming movies and looking up obscure facts essential?
Add to that 5G. Now, we can go about everywhere and make a phone call, and more. That’s because of the wonders of 5G, which are, it turns out more energy efficient than 4G, but the use is higher. The fact is that we can unplug.
The question I ask myself is what the appropriate use of technology is and how much do I need to use it? I challenge myself and my grandchildren to remember landscapes and use a map to find things. And out of protest, I am starting to write in cursive all the time, it’s not illegal yet.

The future of what the elders called the “spider web in the sky” is up to us all, although some folks want to just turn their world over to AI. It is possible to unplug and look at the northern lights. And, maybe we want to just use that renewable energy to keep the lights on in the villages.
While North Dakota politicians pin hopes on the crypto currencies, I’m taking futures in hemp, halal goats, buffalo, and heritage seeds. I might even add some bees into the mix. I’m going to use as little power as needed, look at the skies without light pollution and thank the Grandfathers for what I have, it’s more than enough. And, some mysteries, should just be.
