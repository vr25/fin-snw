In the fast-evolving world of artificial intelligence, OpenAI’s latest move has ignited a firestorm of debate among tech experts, legal scholars, and industry watchers. Reports emerged this week that the San Francisco-based company is soliciting third-party contractors to submit authentic work samples from their previous or current jobs. This initiative, aimed at benchmarking and refining AI models for real-world office tasks, involves uploading files such as documents, spreadsheets, code repositories, and presentations. Contractors are tasked with redacting sensitive information themselves, a process that has raised eyebrows over potential risks to intellectual property and data privacy.
The strategy comes as OpenAI pushes to develop more sophisticated AI agents capable of handling complex professional duties, from drafting reports to analyzing data. According to sources familiar with the matter, this data collection is part of a broader effort to create a “human baseline” for evaluating AI performance. By comparing machine outputs against genuine human work, OpenAI hopes to accelerate the automation of white-collar roles, a goal that aligns with its mission to build artificial general intelligence.
But this approach isn’t without controversy. Intellectual property lawyers have warned that relying on contractors to self-censor confidential details places enormous trust in individuals who may not fully understand the nuances of trade secrets or non-disclosure agreements. One expert described it as a high-stakes gamble, potentially exposing OpenAI to lawsuits if proprietary information slips through.
The Mechanics of OpenAI’s Data Drive
Details of the program surfaced through investigative reporting, revealing that OpenAI has partnered with data vendors like Handshake AI to facilitate the uploads. Contractors are instructed to provide not just descriptions of tasks but the actual deliverables, stripped of personally identifiable or sensitive data. This method allows OpenAI to train its models on diverse, high-quality examples drawn from fields like marketing, software development, and finance.
The initiative builds on OpenAI’s history of aggressive data sourcing. In the past, the company has faced scrutiny for scraping vast amounts of internet data to train models like GPT-4, leading to legal battles with content creators over copyright infringement. Now, by targeting professional work products, OpenAI appears to be seeking more targeted, enterprise-grade datasets that could give its AI an edge in simulating human expertise.
Industry insiders note that this isn’t unique to OpenAI; competitors like Anthropic and Google have explored similar tactics to enhance model accuracy. However, OpenAI’s scale—backed by billions in funding from Microsoft and others—amplifies the implications. If successful, this could hasten the integration of AI into everyday business operations, but at what cost to data ethics?
Legal Perils and Expert Warnings
Evan Brown, an intellectual property lawyer quoted in a recent piece, cautioned that AI labs adopting this tactic are “putting itself at great risk.” As detailed in WIRED, the reliance on contractors to decide what constitutes confidential information introduces vulnerabilities. A single oversight could lead to trade secret misappropriation claims, especially if uploaded materials include proprietary algorithms or business strategies from former employers.
Further coverage from TechCrunch highlights how this fits into OpenAI’s pattern of pushing boundaries. The article points out that while OpenAI declined to comment on specifics, the move underscores the company’s hunger for premium data amid a shortage of publicly available sources. Legal experts argue that without robust verification processes, OpenAI could inadvertently become a repository for stolen intellectual property.
On social platforms like X, sentiment echoes these concerns. Posts from tech professionals express alarm over privacy implications, with some calling it a “shortcut to enterprise data without proper licensing.” One user likened it to “data acquisition disguised as research,” warning that domain-specific patterns from real jobs could train AI without compensating original creators or companies.

Beyond legal risks, ethicists are questioning the broader ramifications for workers. Contractors, often gig economy participants, might feel pressured to share past work to secure ongoing gigs with OpenAI, potentially violating their own NDAs. This dynamic raises issues of consent and exploitation, as individuals weigh job opportunities against professional integrity.
Reports from The Indian Express emphasize that the program is designed to establish benchmarks for AI against human performance across various tasks. Yet, critics argue this commodifies human labor without adequate safeguards. For instance, if a contractor uploads a marketing strategy from a prior role, even redacted, subtle patterns could inform AI models that compete directly with human workers.
Discussions on X amplify these ethical debates, with users pointing to past AI controversies, such as unpaid labor in bug testing for tools like Sora. This sentiment underscores a growing distrust of AI firms’ data practices, where innovation often outpaces ethical frameworks.
