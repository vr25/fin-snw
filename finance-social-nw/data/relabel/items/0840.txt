San Francisco’s Public Utilities Commission has consistently maintained that the two major storms that sent raw sewage and floodwater spewing into Marina district homes in 2021 and 2022 were the result of massive, unprecedented rainfall — and not the fault of The City’s sewer system.
Agency officials said that each deluge was large enough to overwhelm a system not designed to handle such large volumes of water.
But in interviews with The Examiner, a group of current and former SFPUC employees — including engineers with long experience working on The City’s sewers — told a very different story about where responsibility lies for the storm damage.
The group said the extraordinary damage suffered in the Marina can be traced to the May 2021 closure by the SFPUC of the Pierce Street outfall, a century-old pipe structure in the Marina that enabled the discharge of overflow stormwater and sewage into the bay. Outfalls are designed to take pressure off the sewage system during large storms, similar to relief valves.
“I noted that it was quite a coincidence that the Marina had flooded that badly during the very next storm after the Pierce outfall was closed,” said Megan Abadie, a former SFPUC assistant engineer responsible for inspecting The City’s outfalls. “But I wanted to wait until I saw the data to conclude whether the Pierce [Street] outfall closure contributed to the damage. Then, I saw the data and modeling. It was quite clear that the Pierce closure contributed to the damage.”
A current SFPUC staffer and a former San Francisco Department of Public Works employee agreed with Abadie’s assessment of the data collected following each storm. In addition, The Examiner reviewed internal email exchanges that show engineers working at both agencies believed the decision to close the Pierce Street outfall was based on flawed data without carefully considering alternatives or seeking input from engineers most familiar with the system.
Said one SFPUC engineer who asked for anonymity, “Closing the Pierce Street outfall without expanding [flood release] capacity at nearby outfalls should have never been considered.”
The SFPUC did not respond to a request for comment by press time.
In a statement after publication, the agency said that the Pierce Street Outfall project was undertaken after careful consideration. Its purpose, a spokesperson said, “was to decommission a pipe that was in poor structural condition and to provide substantial improvements to the water quality in San Francisco Bay, especially the enclosed Marina, a heavily used area of the waterfront.”
The agency added that hydraulic modeling conducted at the request of the Regional Water Board after the major storms “concluded that flooding would have happened during both the October 2021 and December 2022 storms even if the Pierce Street Outfall decommissioning project had not occurred and the outfall had remained open.”
The skepticism within the SFPUC and Public Works about the Pierce Street outfall closure will likely be of interest to the group of Marina homeowners that filed suit last August. The group — which includes former 49ers star Joe Montana — alleges that negligence by the city and county of San Francisco, the SFPUC and Public Works were responsible for the damage their homes sustained on Oct. 24, 2021, and Dec. 31, 2022.
“City officials ... should have known that decommissioning the Pierce Street outfall would further increase destructive inundations of properties,” the homeowners wrote in their complaint.
Unlike other California coastal cities, San Francisco employs a combined sewer system, which means the same network of pipes collects and treats sewage and stormwater. This two-for-one system enables the city to eliminate pollutants from stormwater before it washes into the Pacific Ocean or San Francisco Bay.
Still, a combined system poses more risks. In large storms, bottlenecks can occur that force sewage — carrying human waste and other unhealthy materials — into streets and homes.
That’s what happened to the Marina in 2021 and 2022 — as well as numerous times in San Francisco’s Mission, West Portal and Lower Alemany districts.

For years, residents in those neighborhoods accused city leaders of dragging their feet when addressing the chronic flooding. Finally, in April 2021, the California Regional Water Quality Control Board issued a cleanup and abatement order, and soon after, the SFPUC agreed to spend $632 million on completing overflow reduction projects to serve those areas.
Due to The City’s aging infrastructure and challenging topography, San Francisco’s sewer system isn’t easy to manage. Some parts of the sewer are more than 100 years old. Upgrades and maintenance are often expensive. Climate change — in the form of increasingly powerful storms — continues to pressure the system.
But controlling costs and protecting ratepayers from dangerous floods are some of the reasons why the decision to close the Pierce Street outfall was the wrong one, according to current and former SFPUC and Public Works employees.
The final decision to close the Pierce Street outfall blindsided some SFPUC and Public Works engineers who worked most closely with San Francisco’s outfalls. They were not included in that decision.
They were also surprised to learn the analysis used for this decision did not consider options for increasing flood-relief capacity at nearby outfalls to offset the capacity lost with the Pierce Street outfall closure, nor did the studies include alternatives to closing Pierce. SFPUC staff who spoke to The Examiner, including Abadie, say all of these choices were presented at various project team meetings beginning years before the final decision.
In a Feb. 13, 2019 email, SPFUC principal engineer Jon Loiacono emailed colleague Ed Ho and said that upgrading the Pierce Street outfall “might be better than removing the outfall entirely.”
Abadie, considered by many of her SFPUC coworkers to be among the agency’s most knowledgeable engineers on outfalls, appeared disturbed in email conversations later that week about the data used to support an argument for closing the Pierce Street outfall.
“Wow, they did not even look at any larger storms beyond [level of service], let alone address redundancy or cost of rehab,” Abadie wrote. “Even if this particular decision to close Pierce proves ultimately OK, [the assessment] you forwarded is a severely incomplete model/precedent for evaluating outfall closures in general ... the decision framework that was used for closing Pierce has some serious holes in it, which could lead to big problems if applied to other [outfalls].”
“Level of service” is a term used by public agencies when establishing metrics that determine service requirements. Whether anyone who participated in the February 2019 email chain shared concerns with SFPUC or Public Works’ leadership is unclear.
Abadie acknowledges that after the final decision to shut down the Pierce Street outfall was circulated, she did not argue against the closure or express fears about potential flooding in the Marina with anyone in management. She said she doesn’t know whether anyone else at the agencies approached management with concerns.
By the time the final decision was made, Abadie said she felt those with more authority — including Saed Toloui, project manager, and Greg Norby, assistant general manager of the SFPUC’s Wastewater and Stormwater Enterprise — were already determined to shut down the outfall.
In the months and even years prior to the final decision, however, Abadie said that she and other colleagues expressed their beliefs to those in leadership that the SFPUC should explore alternatives to closure or search for ways to pair closing the outfall with a simultaneous expansion of capacity at other outfalls.
Another trend that Abadie said discouraged her from speaking out was the pressure that some managers put on her to close outfalls in general.
“I felt pressured to recommend closing down other outfalls,” Abadie told The Examiner. “Anytime there was an outfall that needed repairs, Saed would ask me if I would recommend closing it down. Then it was on me to go to bat and become the spokesperson for the outfalls.
“I don’t know why he wanted to close them down. The outfalls were paid for long ago by ratepayers. The thing is, no one wants to think about them until they’re needed.”
