The multi-billion-dollar debate: Big tech spending on AI vs. the actual return
Article content
As “big tech” ramps up spending for the next phase of growth, rising capital intensity could bring with it relatively more earnings cyclicality. This could have implications for the broader equity market as big tech (defined as Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, Meta and Alphabet) now make ups roughly 30 per cent of the S&P 500 and almost 20 per cent of the Morgan Stanley Composite Index, (MSCI) All-Country World Index. Capital expenditures (capex) for big tech totaled roughly $56bn in Q2 2024, a 56 per cent jump from the same quarter in 2023 (~$36bn). This surge has driven the capex-to-sales ratio to the highest level in a decade amid the AI buildout.
Amongst big tech, the three largest “hyperscalers” (Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet which together account for 70 per cent+ of global cloud market share) have boosted their capex by the most.

Big tech’s growing capex outlays come at a time when the group will be lapping tougher year-over-year comparables over the next 12 months following the accelerated growth phase in from the second half of 2023 to the fist half of 2024. Notably, the two previous spikes in capex over the past decade (in 2018 and 2022), preceded earnings slowdowns, suggesting heightened cyclicality as sales growth moderated from elevated levels.
This means the ability to deliver on fundamentals will be crucial to maintain premium valuations within big tech. In the two previous “rising-spend, slowing-growth” episodes in 2018 and 2022, forward price earnings (P/E) ratios decreased to the low 20 times. Big tech currently trades at a forward P/E of 28 times, modestly below the average of 29x since 2013, but still notably higher than the S&P 500’s 22x. While high current valuations can dampen future returns, big tech’s valuation premium largely reflect investors’ confidence in sustained long-term earnings growth and hasn’t prevented the group from outperforming the broad market over the past ten years.
For now, markets expect the fundamental momentum for big tech to carry into next year, with revenues and EPS projected to grow in the high teens in 2025. To maintain the valuation premium relative to the broad market, however, the group will likely need to consistently meet/exceed these expectations and demonstrate it can continue to grow faster than the broader market. Beyond that, the regulatory backdrop (e.g., antitrust enforcement) is also worth monitoring from a sentiment perspective, as it is one of few areas where there is bipartisan political agreement in the U.S. against big tech.
Crucially, the return on invested capital (ROIC) and return on equity (ROE) have continued to improve for big tech. Average ROIC for big tech is currently north of 40 per cent. The group also continues to demonstrate an ability to produce substantially higher ROE than the broad market. The key issue, however in my mind, is whether big tech’s growth rates are sustainable over time, and even more importantly, does the group begin to show a ROIC to investors. The answer to this last question will determine technology valuations for 2025 and beyond. Stay tuned!
