GRAND FORKS — As a professional wedding officiant, Amber Olsen has seen it all.
Like the "Game of Thrones" wedding, with members of the wedding party clad in elaborate cloaks, groomsmen brandishing swords and the bride gliding down a pebble-paved path to the theme song from the popular medieval fantasy.

Or the quick, no-frills ceremonies in which couples legally cement their unions by signing documents at a coffee shop, then grab lattes and head out on their honeymoon.
Or the bride and groom who replaced the traditional unity candle ceremony with a condiment ceremony, during which they commemorated their first date by pouring ketchup and mustard on a sandwich and sharing it.
"I've married about 400 couples, representing a variation from the coffee shop wedding to the very fabulous, full-blown wedding with 300 to 400 guests to everything in between," said Olsen, whose business is named Officiant Amber. "It's just a passion of mine and something that I absolutely love to do."
Olsen has proven to be such a savvy entrepreneur that she includes services like marriage-license guidance and legal name-change expertise, wedding coordination and pre-marriage coaching.
It's the key difference between someone who leads a wedding after simply getting ordained online and the professional officiant, who charges for their services and continually hones their craft. Olsen estimates there are around four professional officiants currently doing business in Fargo and Grand Forks.
“The common misconception is, ‘I’m not going to pay someone to stand up and talk for 15 minutes,’ but people don’t realize the amount of work that goes into this,” the Grand Forks resident and part-time substitute teacher said. "The officiant is an expert, a professional who is there to usher the couple through the whole process."
Indeed, Olsen approaches her work with the zeal of someone who was born to do this. The former WDAZ reporter thoroughly interviews her prospective brides and grooms on everything from what brought them together to the type of unity ceremony that most perfectly fits them. She then weaves the couple's story into a meaningful ceremony.

Her conscientious approach has earned her WeddingWire's Couple Choice award every year since 2018. She's also earned 100% five-star ratings on her Facebook page, WeddingWire.com and Google. She is proud that these ratings came directly from couples, even as she realizes a less-conventional service sometimes ruffles the feathers of traditionalists.
“It’s not everyone’s cup of tea,” she said. “I’ve heard sometimes after the ceremony, ‘Well, that’s not the way it’s always been done.’ And that’s OK. It’s not the way everyone has done it, but for the couple who chose to be married this way, it was perfect for them.’”
Raised on Disney; in love with romance
The South Dakota native admits she's always been intrigued by marriage, and planned her own 2010 nuptials with the precision of a diamond cutter.
After graduating from South Dakota State in 2006, she worked as a TV reporter and director and a radio deejay — occupations that prepared her well for the public speaking and storytelling essential to delivering a wedding ceremony. But regardless of what she did professionally, her interests always returned to the idea of loving couples riding happily into the sunset. "I grew up watching Disney movies, so that kind of fairy-tale love is something that always really intrigued me," she said.
Over eight years ago, she attended her brother's wedding, which featured a professional officiant. "I thought, 'Wow, this person gets to tell people's love stories and gets to know them as a couple and individuals in order to stand up in front of all of their closest friends and family and share their love story," she recalls.
That night, she went online to the American Marriage Ministries website and filled out the form to become ordained. She and her husband had relocated to California, which was slightly ahead of the Upper Midwest in the professional officiant trend. With journalistic zeal, she dove head-first into researching the industry, networked with other wedding vendors and eventually joined an international wedding officiant organization.
Her first wedding took place in the back of a Mexican restaurant, where most of the guests didn't speak English. "I remember feeling nervous and excited, and at same time feeling like this makes a difference — not only for this couple but also for the community, because essentially what we’re doing is helping to create families," she said.

California weddings were a bit more adventurous than in the more staid Midwest, which meant leading theme weddings like a Harry Potter marriage ceremony, in which she was asked to bless the rings with a "magic" wand. Olsen always rolled with the flow, wearing whatever costume was required and incorporating appropriate verbiage from "Game of Thrones'" notorious "Red Wedding" into the script.
When your love is ablaze - literally
When the Olsens returned to North Dakota in 2019 to raise their two young children, Olsen continued honing her craft and networked with other wedding vendors. "It's a pretty tight-knit little community," she said. "It's all of us working together as a big family and team for the couple."
In the Red River Valley, most of her weddings are at event centers, parks and the occasional church. But some couples choose venues that carry a special meaning to their relationship. Last year, she married a couple under a specific tree on a Grand Forks bike path. The sweethearts had taken the path on their first date, and that was the very spot where the woman almost choked on a mouthful of food until her quick-thinking future husband saved her.
Even traditional wedding venues can attract unexpected drama. In the winter of 2020, Mitchell Maanum and Hayley Anderson booked their wedding at the Ivy + Rose Warehouse, 22 14 1/2 St. N., Fargo. Anderson was ready to walk down the aisle when the event manager rushed over to tell her the big building right across the street was in flames.
After calling 911, the couple was assured that firefighters were battling the blaze and it would be safe to tie the knot. They did so in front of a small group of immediate family plus friends tuning in via livestream. It created an especially vivid ceremony, as the bay window where they stood gazed out at a fire that seemed to turn the whole sky red.
By the time they finished the ceremony, "the flames were huge," Olsen recalls. "The firetrucks had arrived, and were putting out the fire, so we had everyone move away from the big bay windows in case they blew out, and the firemen had everyone contained in the building until they determined it was safe to move our vehicles."
Then again, neither snow nor fire nor driving rain will stop a good officiant. "I've also performed ceremonies in the pouring rain, " Olsen said. "There was a wedding (near Fargo) last summer, where it was a torrential downpour. The bride was adamant about continuing the ceremony outside, but since it was raining so hard, the guests came in from the rain and sat at their tables, while the bridal party, the couple and myself stood just outside of the huge doors in the pouring rain and did the ceremony."

Officiant laws change by county
To ensure her couples get a ceremony that represents them, she will give them a detailed questionnaire early on to find out how they met, what makes them work as a couple and how they envision their future together. This helps her tell their stories in the ceremony script, which the couple can review and modify until they're satisfied.
"Ultimately, what I’m going for is 'We love it, this is our story, this is who we are, this is what I envisioned for my wedding day,'" Olsen said. "My husband always teases me that if I can get laughter or tears from the couple, I know I’m doing my job.”
Olsen's main concern is that the couple is comfortable with everything from the content of the script to the flow of the event. "Some professional officiants, they love the surprise element of the ceremony," she said. "I'm not that way. I'm a planner."
She's also created two complementary products, including a comprehensive name-change packet ($60) and a book titled, "Navigating Your Wedding Ceremony" ($20) to guide couples through the more technical aspects of wedding-planning. The name-change packet includes every application that is needed for every agency, in both Minnesota and North Dakota, to legally change one's name.
She also will provide expertise to couples on the different requirements to legally officiate a wedding, as rules change according to state and county.
"In some counties you have to pre-register with the county or state. Every county is so different, so a marriage in Clay will be completely different than in Cass. So it's just understanding all the legalities that come with the wedding itself," she said.
Her newest service is pre-marriage coaching, in which couples figure out their positions on big issues like child-rearing, how they settle differences or money-management.

Olsen is a certified pre-marriage coach through the Prepare Enrich program. Couples who complete it will receive a $75 discount on their Minnesota marriage license.
On her website, she offers different marriage packages, beginning with The Kiss, a $150 "elopement package," in which the couple doesn't want a ceremony but meets her at a coffee shop or park so they can sign the necessary papers to be legally married.
Or they can splurge on an all-inclusive ceremony package, which includes a custom ceremony, the name-change packet, a wedding rehearsal, her book and four premarital coaching sessions for $1,375.
"I love being able to help the couple and ease their fears, such as telling them where to get their marriage license, how to get through the name change process and all the minor details of the ceremony themselves, so it's all going to go as fluidly as possible," Olsen said. "You only get one shot to do down the aisle on that day."
