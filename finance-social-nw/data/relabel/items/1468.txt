I. Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) has quickly become a springboard for breakthroughs in personalized medicine, enhanced medical imaging, and predictive modeling for drug development.[1] And given the role it played in two recent Nobel Prizes—one in chemistry, and the other in physics—AI appears to have an exceedingly bright future as a driver of innovation.
Correspondingly, AI will likely be a central limitation in many future patent claims. New treatments should lead to new patent protections. Yet a recent Federal Circuit decision and a patent office guidance document highlight a potential continuing hurdle for patents that claim AI advances in medical treatments: a lack of patentable subject matter.
Determining how to navigate this new terrain will be essential for innovators and businesses that want to protect inventive applications of AI in medical treatments. This post offers a quick guide through the relevant issues and possible solutions.
II. Patentable Subject Matter—The Basics
An inventor may only receive a patent for eligible inventions under 35 U.S.C. § 101. For a patent to issue, it must claim a “new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter.”[2] To assess patent eligibility under Section 101, courts apply the canonical two-step Alice/Mayo test.[3] The first step assesses whether a claim is directed to categorically ineligible subject matter, such as abstract ideas, laws of nature, or mental processes.[4] Claims not directed to ineligible subject matter are sufficiently patentable, while inventions that fall into one of these categories move to step two of the Alice/Mayo test.
Step two assesses whether the claim elements—individually, or in combination—add something to the ineligible concept to transform it into patentable subject matter. That is, as the Mayo Court put it, “do the patent claims add enough to their statements of [natural laws] to allow the processes they describe to qualify as patent eligible processes that apply natural laws?”[5] To apply that natural law or abstract idea, the claim must practically impose a “meaningful limit” on that law or idea.[6] Courts may also analyze the claim for an “inventive concept”—a concept that adds something “significantly more” than the natural law or abstract idea itself, such that the claim transforms that law or idea.[7]
At first glance, the use of AI might seem to satisfy the Alice/Mayo test. It provides either a “meaningful limit” (a particular implementation of conventional computer technology) or an “inventive concept” (an innovation on a computer’s information processing and output capability). Yet, in the context of medical treatment, a recent decision sharpens the notion that AI may fail this critical test.
III. AI Visualize, Inc. v. Nuance Commc’ns, Inc.[8]—A Cautionary Tale for AI
AI Visualize involved a patent infringement suit between two AI-based healthcare companies: AI Visualize and Nuance. AI Visualize asserted patents that claimed a method for creating an on-demand 3D image of a medical scan—compiled from 2D images—without downloading the large image to a local device.[9] AI Visualize describes this technology as AI and cloud computing advancements in medical image visualization.[10] In its briefs, AI Visualize argued its patents were limited to solving the difficult issue of viewing medical scans without excess computer power or access to high-speed links.[11]
The Federal Circuit, however, disagreed. The court affirmed invalidation of AI Visualize’s patents because the claims were directed to the abstract idea of converting data and displaying it via a computer.[12] Looking to possible “inventive concepts,” the Court did not find the patents contributed significantly more than a conventional computer.[13] The district court below had come to a similar conclusion. It ruled that the innovative technology improved the accessing of remote data, not the computer’s technical functionality.[14]
The Federal Circuit’s decision draws attention to two key takeaways. First, AI should not be seen as improving upon conventional computer functionality for purposes of patentability. The AI Visualize Court did not treat the AI and cloud computing elements in the patents as a sufficiently inventive concept. This treatment should come as no surprise; the Federal Circuit recently concluded that machine learning is likewise patent-ineligible, as discussed by MoFo’s Tech Group.[15]
Even dating back to 2015, the Chicago-based Northern District of Illinois analogized an “artificial neural network” to a “basic computer’s brain.”[16] In the following decade, other district courts have similarly concluded that AI, in and of itself, does not provide an inventive concept.[17] The AI Visualize decision helps crystallize that AI’s inclusion alone in a medical process does not constitute patentable subject matter.
Second, the patents did not claim the purported technical advancements of AI. The Court refused to entertain AI Visualize’s “technical solution to a technical problem” argument because the “technical solution” appeared only in the patent specification, not the claims.[18] Other courts, though, have not necessarily followed AI Visualize, relying instead on the specification to inform the scope of the patent claims.[19] Despite the conflicting uses of the specification, the AI Visualize result should still imbue patent seekers with caution when drafting their specification and claims.
IV. The USPTO’s AI Guidance[20]—A Narrow Path Forward
Last summer, the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) published a handful of examples analyzing the patent eligibility of AI inventions.[21] The USPTO has covered other AI issues, such as AI’s use in USPTO practice,[22] and it has continued to actively address emerging issues like AI-assisted inventions.[23] But this guidance is the USPTO’s most recent statement on patent eligibility.
From this guidance, Example #49 stands out. The exemplary patent provides two claims describing a method for treating glaucoma. The first recites administering a glaucoma treatment based on a risk score calculated by AI.[24] The second claim depends from the first claim, requiring that the administered treatment is a particular eyedrop.[25]
The USPTO suggests that claim 1 would be invalid. Although it claims a process, the claimed process involves a patent-ineligible “mental process” of identifying high-risk patients.[26] The generic “administration of a treatment” does not impose a meaningful limit on the mental process, either. Of note, AI did not affect this eligibility calculus. AI, the USPTO warns, “is no improvement to the functioning of a computer.”[27]
On the other hand, the USPTO finds that claim 2 would be valid. The critical difference between the two claims stems from the specificity of the administered treatment.[28] Whereas claim 1 only claims an abstract treatment of glaucoma, claim 2 limits treatment to a particular eyedrop. The direction to use the particular eyedrop is the key to making this medical treatment eligible subject matter.
Not relevant, still, to the claim’s eligibility is the AI limitation.[29] The USPTO’s analysis supports the reasoning in AI Visualize and similar district court opinions, and perhaps extends them by characterizing AI as merely refining an abstract idea.[30] It is not clear from the guidance alone what the USPTO would deem a sufficiently inventive use of AI in a medical treatment.
V. Moving Forward—How to Navigate the Ever-Changing AI Playing Field
The current landscape is shifting towards patent challengers. The combination of AI Visualize and the USPTO’s guidance broadly supports the ineligibility of patents that hinge on AI limitations. When threatened with infringing a patent utilizing AI for a medical treatment, patent challengers should have a lack of patentable subject matter defense in their toolbox.
But this defense is not automatic, nor is the law surrounding AI’s patent eligibility settled. For example, courts have found “inventive concepts” using AI in medical treatments in ways that run counter to the USPTO’s guidance.[31] Some practitioners have also identified flaws in the USPTO’s guidance—such as improperly excluding analysis of claim elements in combination—that could limit the guidance’s utility.[32]
The USPTO’s policy may also change, if not seismically shift, in response to the current presidential administration. Principally, USPTO director nominee John Squires has long been supportive of strong patent protections, including expanding patent eligibility to remain technologically competitive with powers like China.[33] President Trump has further announced policies intended to “remove barriers” to AI development, causing the USPTO to revoke at least one AI strategy document.[34] To date, the USPTO has not reversed course on its eligibility guidance, but the specter of a possible change may still loom over upcoming patent filings and challenges. Removing barriers to AI development could signal less patent protection to encourage competition, while more investment in AI could allow for additional protectable inventions.
A change in the law is also possible. The Senate has introduced legislation—the Patent Eligibility Restoration Act of 2025—to remove certain judicial exceptions from the subject matter eligibility framework, such as abstract ideas.[35] Scholars have also contemplated more sweeping changes to adapt patent law to the unique innovative space AI occupies. One such solution would have courts analyze AI’s eligibility based on its “layers” of data, software, and application,[36] while another would introduce a separate statutory carve-out for AI eligibility based on the AI invention’s output.[37]
Turning back to the present day, patent defendants still have the upper hand. That means patentees should address hurdles at the drafting stage, if possible, before the patents ever reach litigation. Both MoFo blog posts cited above provide advice to applicants prosecuting AI patents, such as utilizing examiner interviews to highlight specific technical advances or identifying the specific applications or actions of the claimed technical improvement. Without taking these critical steps, patentees will likely bear the brunt of the trend of invalidating patents relating to AI innovation in medical treatment.
